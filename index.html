A Megakernel per Model

Coding agents wrote an inference engine for one checkpoint on one card at a time. Where it beats vLLM and SGLang, and where it does not.

Nov 15, 2026 · interim results · every number on this page is generated from job logs · all measurements and every run

TL;DR

General inference engines such as vLLM and SGLang serve every model through one scheduler and one library of kernels. We asked what happens when the engine is instead written for a single checkpoint on a single card, as one GPU kernel that never hands control back to the host between tokens (a megakernel), and when a coding agent writes it. For five open models, from 8 B to 594 B parameters, an agent in an autoresearch loop wrote such an engine against an evaluator it could not change: 10 runs, 242 agent-hours, 356 evaluated submissions.

Steady decode. With the checkpoint's own number formats (track A), the engines are 1.08–1.43× as fast as the fastest general engine with one sequence on all five models, and at least 1.10× with both one and eight sequences on 2 of them (Qwen3-8B and Inkling-2-Small). Serving a production trace on Inkling-2-Small and four B200 cards, at 1 to 4 requests per second, 100% of requests stay within the service levels against 83–85% for SGLang, and the longest stall is 0.08–0.09 s against 2.3–2.8 s.

It is not a free win. With eight sequences two models are at or below the general engine, the serving engine's median time between tokens is three to four times SGLang's and it saturates first, and the steady-decode engines hold 8 sequences and 2,048 tokens of context and nothing more. 11 of the 13 final engines were audited by an independent agent; none broke the rules, and three reported results had to be corrected.

One agent, one model, one card

A run gives a coding agent (Claude, in Claude Code, with nobody in the loop) a checkpoint, a card and a budget of agent-hours. The agent writes CUDA; a command sends the source to an evaluator the agent can neither read nor change. The evaluator builds it, compares its logits with a float64 reference of the model position by position, checks the greedy tokens, and then times it behind an HTTP server with the same client that timed the general engines. Only a submission that passes gets a number.

Results are kept on separate tracks. Track A keeps the checkpoint's number formats and data flow: it is the like-for-like comparison. Track B allows model-specific changes that leave the outputs identical, such as storing BF16 weights losslessly in 12 bits. Decoding several tokens per model pass is a third track and is reported apart.

Every run converged on the same kind of object: one kernel per card that is launched once and stays resident, with the layers, the choice of the next token and the loop over tokens inside it, and the weights laid out in the order the tensor cores read them. Nothing on the GPU waits for a host scheduler between tokens.

Steady decode: faster on every model with one sequence

The workload is the one the general engines are tuned for: a 64-token prompt, 1,024 new tokens, greedy, one or eight sequences that start together. Each system is served in turn on the same card and timed by the same client over HTTP, three rounds.

With one sequence track A is ahead on all five models, by 1.08–1.43×. With eight the margin shrinks, and the bar we set ourselves, 1.10× at both batch sizes, is met only on Qwen3-8B and Inkling-2-Small: North Mini Code (1.02×), flint (0.98×), Qwen3.8-27B (1.04×) fall short of it.

Track B adds 1.45× and 1.53× on Qwen3-8B and 1.18× and 1.16× on Qwen3.8-27B, both from 12-bit weights whose outputs are bit-identical to track A's. The track-B engines of North and flint are a different thing: they keep intermediate values in float32 instead of BF16, which is at least as accurate but no longer the same outputs, so we do not call them lossless. Sampling instead of greedy decoding costs both sides 1 to 11% and leaves the ratios where they were. Multi-token decoding on Qwen3.8-27B, exact draft-and-verify, runs at 3.71 ms per token on natural text, 2.4× its own track A.

Serving a production trace: no stalls, at a price

Steady decode is the easy case. In the Mooncake trace of tool and agent traffic, prompts of 9,000 tokens on average arrive while other requests are decoding, and a general engine's decoding streams stall while a long prompt is processed. For two models a second run turned the steady-decode engine into one that takes requests as they arrive, processes prompts in chunks inside the same kernel, pages its own K/V memory and reuses shared prefixes.

At 1, 2 and 4 requests per second the megakernel keeps 99.9–100.0% of requests within the service levels, SGLang 82.7–85.4%. The difference is the tail: the 99th-percentile time between a request's tokens is 68–72 ms against 697–796 ms, and the longest stall 0.08–0.09 s against 2.3–2.8 s.

The price is everywhere else. The median time between tokens is 28–44 ms against 9–10 ms, the 99th-percentile first token 3.4–3.9 s against 2.2 s, and at 8 requests per second the engine falls behind: 34% within the service levels, with a median first token after 7.6 s, where SGLang still holds 82%. The audit traced all of this to one design decision. Every pass over the model, whether it carries one decoding row or 3,584 prompt positions, runs the same program in the same summation order, and its size is capped by the time budget of the rows that are decoding. That is what removes the stalls and makes a request's tokens independent of its neighbours. It also makes plain steady decode 2.4× slower than SGLang (11.4 against 4.7 ms per token), three times slower than the steady-decode engine it grew out of.

The earlier serving run, Qwen3-8B on one H100, shows the same shape at its two rates: 89–97% within the service levels against 59–74% for vLLM and SGLang, a longest stall of 0.09 s against 0.6–7.4 s, and a worse 99th-percentile first token (7–16 s against 6–12 s).

Where the engines stop

An engine written for one workload does that workload. Moving one variable at a time away from it shows how narrow the steady-decode engines are, and what the serving engines gave up to be wider.

Checking engines an agent wrote

A fast engine that computes something else is worth nothing, and here the author is an optimizer with a score to raise. So each final engine went through checks the agent never saw, and then to an independent agent whose only job was to find what is wrong with it: it read the source, built variants and ran its own jobs. The two Qwen3-8B serving engines are the exception: they passed their run's evaluator and have not been audited.

The audits found no engine that calls another engine, caches outputs, recognises the evaluator's inputs or skips model work. They did change three results:

  • Multi-token decoding looked 9× better than it is. The evaluator reported 0.415 ms per token on Qwen3.8-27B. Two of the three timed prompts send the model into a two-token loop that the drafts predict almost perfectly; on natural text the engine runs at 3.71 ms.
  • Two track-B engines are not lossless. flint's differs from its own track-A build at every one of 14,336 positions checked, and picks another top token at 10.8% of them. The label was the agent's, and the evaluator did not test it.
  • Silent failure past the context limit. Beyond about 2,000 positions the Inkling steady-decode engine overwrote other sequences' K/V memory and flint's returned zero tokens, both without an error. The serving engine checks its limits, but has an out-of-bounds write when built for fewer than 64 sequences.

Each of these passed the run's evaluator. Its test prompts were synthetic, it checked the served tokens only loosely, it took the track label on trust, and its float64 reference stopped at 1,152 positions.

What's next

The two kinds of engine have to become one: a serving engine whose small passes are as fast as the steady-decode kernel would keep the stall-free tail without the threefold cost at the median. The evaluation changes first, with natural-text prompts, exact token checks on the served path, a bit-for-bit gate for track B and a reference that reaches 32,000 positions. flint's run has been extended to go after eight sequences.
